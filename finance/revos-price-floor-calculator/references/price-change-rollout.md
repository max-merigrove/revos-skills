# Rolling out a price change

Adapted for service businesses from the price change rollout in marketingskills by Corey Haines (MIT; notice in LICENSE.md), and from Merigrove's own pricing practice. Read this when an offer's verdict is raise or restructure and the founder asks how to make the change.

A price change is a rollout, not a switch.

## 1. Check the floor and the demand

The calculator gives the break-even client loss for each rise in two views, cash first. In plain cash: the share beyond which the business takes in less until the hours are refilled. Counting freed hours at the founder's rate: the larger share they could lose and be no worse off, but only if they refill those hours at their rate (not shown for an offer sold once a month or less). If the founder expects to lose fewer than the cash line, the rise pays in cash from the first month.

Demand says when, as well as whether. Raise for new clients first when any of these is true: the diary is full; most calls with a qualified buyer end in a yes; there is a waiting list. Each says demand is ahead of supply at the current price, and a rise is the cheapest capacity a founder can add. These are judgements from the founder's own diary, not thresholds to quote.

## 2. New clients first

New clients have no old price to compare against and no relationship at stake, so they tell the founder cleanly whether the number holds. Change the price on the proposal template, the website and the next call, and watch what happens over the next few proposals.

## 3. Then existing clients, a few at a time

Move a small group first and watch for a billing cycle before widening. A staggered move leaves a way back if the reaction is worse than expected.

## 4. Grandfathering is a transition, not an exemption

If some clients stay on the old price for a while, set the date it ends. A client left at the old price for good is a subsidy that grows every year, and the calculator shows its size: the gap between the old price and the floor, each month.

## 5. Say why, in advance, with something generous attached

The reason is usually true and simple: the work delivered now is not the work that was priced when the client joined, or the costs behind it have risen. Give notice. Offer a softening route: the old price held for anyone who commits to a longer term now, or a final month at the old rate.

## 6. Expect to lose some clients

The clients most likely to leave over a justified rise are usually the least profitable and the most demanding, which is why the break-even test runs on contribution rather than revenue. Freed hours are worth the founder's rate.

## 7. Restructure without saying "less"

When the verdict is restructure, the price stays and the hours come down. Make the change visible as a clearer offer, not a cut: a stated number of revision rounds, a set call rhythm, a defined monthly allowance with extras priced, a templated start. Write the scope down so the hours stay down.

## 8. Discounts

Discounts are sized against what a sale leaves, not against the price. A discount of a few per cent on an offer with a thin contribution can remove most of what the sale leaves. Set a limit once and hold it.

## What is not here

Notice letters, the call script and answers to pushback are a separate job. This skill gives the numbers and the options; it does not write the letter.
