# What to say: pricing extra work without friction

The client asked for something. The founder wants to say yes and be paid for it, and keep the client liking them. This file is how the replies get written. Read it in full before writing any message.

## 1. The stance

A request for extra work is good news. It means the client trusts the founder with more. The reply treats it that way: it says yes to the thing they want, puts a price or a trade on it, and makes the next step one word from the client.

Three beliefs sit under every reply:

- **A price is information, not a confrontation.** "That's $1,200" is no ruder than "that's Thursday". Delivered plainly, it reads as competence.
- **The founder is not in trouble.** Nobody apologises for having a price. An apology tells the client the price is negotiable and the founder feels guilty about it.
- **The client chooses.** Two or three clear options give the client control. A client who chooses the swap does not feel refused; a client who chooses to pay does not feel sold to.

What the reply never does: lecture about scope, quote the agreement, point at the client's past requests, or explain why the founder deserves to be paid.

## 2. The three options, and when each one wins

Every open request gets price and next phase. The swap is written only when `compute` lists an equal-effort swap, or the founder swaps part of a deliverable whose share comes out equal (`swap_hours`, such as `"=10*6/12"` for six of twelve posts). A swap that is not equal effort is a discount in disguise, and the build rejects it. One option is marked recommended, with a one-line reason.

| Option | What it says | Wins when |
|---|---|---|
| **Price it** | Yes, here is what it takes and costs, here is when it lands | Default for a strong relationship; the request is clearly new work; the client has budget; the founder has capacity |
| **Swap it** | Yes, and to keep the fee the same, it replaces a listed item of the same effort this month | The relationship is fragile or price-sensitive; an equal-effort deliverable exists (the script lists candidates); the client values the new thing above a routine one |
| **Next phase** | Yes, it goes first in next month's plan, or into a second phase, priced or swapped then | The client is about to renew (fold it into the renewal); the founder is at capacity this month; the request is a bigger idea that deserves its own brief |
| **Scope it first** | Yes, and before a firm price, a short written plan or spec that pins down what it involves | `compute` says "scope it first": the build is at least as big as the month's planned hours (or half a project), so one confident figure would be a guess |

A fifth, **use the goodwill allowance**, applies only when the founder chose an allowance and the request fits inside it. It is still said out loud ("this one comes out of the two goodwill hours this month"), so the client sees the gift instead of assuming the work is free.

**Ranges.** When the founder gave the hours as a range ("30-40 hrs", "3 to 4 days"), the price reply quotes both ends ("$4,875 to $6,500"), never the middle as if it were firm. The ledger counts the middle and says so.

The script suggests a recommendation from the founder's policy and relationship. A saved learning (for example "picks the swap for long-standing clients") overrides it; say so in the reason.

### Estimates and ranges

A range the founder gave is quoted with both ends. When the founder said it could run higher ("maybe more", "30-40+"), the reply says the range is an estimate before scoping, not a cap ("that's my early estimate before scoping, not a cap"), so the top figure is never read later as a promise. A deadline the client named is answered honestly from the founder's figures: whether it fits, and what the first phase would be if it does not.

## 3. Anatomy of a good reply

Two to five sentences. Readable on a phone. Five parts, in this order, each usually one sentence:

1. **Yes to the goal.** Name what the client is trying to achieve, in their words where possible. ("A landing page for the spring menu is a good idea.")
2. **Where it sits.** One plain clause, no blame: "It's outside the monthly plan" or "It's a new piece rather than part of this month's posts." Never the first sentence.
3. **The offer, with its figure.** Hours or effort, the exact price from the calculator in the founder's currency, the date it lands in the founder's date style. ("It's about six hours, so £570, and I can have it live by the 14th.") If the founder writes their own fee with tax words ("+ VAT", "+ GST"), add the same words after the price and nothing more: never a rate or a with-tax figure.
4. **The alternative, if there is one.** One sentence, so the client has a choice without a menu. (Optional in the price option; required in the swap.)
5. **The one-word next step.** "Want me to go ahead?" "Which would you prefer?" (Or the founder's own close, if their message shows one.)

Rules the script checks: the price option states the calculator's price exactly (both ends for a range), in the founder's number style ("1.070 €" to a founder who writes "9.800,00 €"); the swap option names the deliverable it replaces and is equal effort; the next-phase option says when; the scope option names the scoping step; no reply opens with a refusal; no apology; no hedge such as "Is that okay for you?"; no legal words; at most 80 words and five sentences.

## 4. Tone by relationship, and reading right in every market

| Relationship | Tone | Lean |
|---|---|---|
| **Strong** | Direct, light, friendly. Short. | Price it. The client already trusts the founder's judgement. |
| **Fragile** (complaints, slow payment, a recent wobble) | Warmer, a touch more explanation of the benefit, never defensive. Keep the price plain. | Swap it where a candidate exists, so the fee does not move during a wobble. |
| **About to renew** | Forward-looking. Use the request as evidence the client wants more from the work. | Next phase, folded into the renewal proposal, priced there. |

Voice comes from the founder's own message if they pasted one: their greeting, sign-off, sentence length, whether they use first names, contractions and "we" or "I", their spelling ("color" or "colour") and their idiom. Copy their habits; never add an exclamation mark they would not use, and never "correct" their English to another country's.

Without a sample, write plain, warm English that reads naturally to an American and a British client alike: contractions, first person, short sentences, no corporate phrasing, and no regional markers. Read the reply three times before it goes in the kit, once as a client in the US, once in the UK and once in a third market (Singapore, Australia or wherever the founder's client is), and change any line that would sound foreign to one of them.

| Reads as one country's | Neutral |
|---|---|
| "Shall I book it in?" | "Want me to go ahead?" |
| "Good shout", "Cheers", "Brilliant" | "Good idea", "Thanks", "Great" |
| "Awesome", "Reach out", "Circle back", "Y'all" | "Great", "Get in touch", "Come back to", "You" |
| "Speak Thursday", "Pop it over" | "Talk on Thursday", "Send it over" |
| A date written 3/9 or 9/3 | The founder's own style; "3 September" or "September 3" when unsure |

## 5. Openers, middles and closes that work

Openers (yes to the goal):
- "Love the idea of a spring landing page."
- "Happy to help with the investor deck."
- "Good idea to add a reel for the launch."
- "Yes, we can get the newsletter going."

Middles (where it sits, then the offer):
- "It's a new piece rather than part of the monthly plan, so I'd price it separately: about six hours, £570."
- "That sits outside this month's posts. It's roughly a day's work, $960, and I could start on Monday."
- "It would take about four hours. I can do it for S$600, or swap it in for the second blog post this month so the fee stays the same."

Closes (one-word next step):
- "Want me to go ahead?"
- "Want me to get started?"
- "Which works better for you?"
- "Say the word and I'll start Monday."

## 6. Phrases that cost the founder money, with rewrites

| Instead of | Write | Why |
|---|---|---|
| "Unfortunately that's outside our scope." | "That's a new piece rather than part of the plan, so here's what it would take." | Regret before a price invites a haggle |
| "I'm afraid I'll have to charge for this." | "It's about six hours, so £570." | The price is information, not bad news |
| "Sorry, but..." | (delete) | Nobody apologises for having a price |
| "As per our contract..." / "Per the agreement..." | "Based on what we agreed in the plan..." (or leave it out) | Legal words turn a conversation into a dispute |
| "This is scope creep." | (never in a client message) | Blames the client for wanting more of the founder's work |
| "You keep asking for extras." | (never) | A telling-off. The ledger is for the founder, not the client |
| "I hope that's OK?" / "Does that make sense?" | "Want me to go ahead?" | A hedge invites a no |
| "Just this once I'll do it for free." | Use the allowance line, said out loud, or price it | "Just this once" sets the next expectation |
| "Our policy is..." | "The way I work is..." | The founder is a person, not a department |
| "I can't do that." | "Here's how we can do that." | Every reply has a price or a trade, never only a refusal |

## 7. The reset note (when extras have already been given away)

When the founder has absorbed extras, the problem is the pattern, not one request. Before answering the next request, the founder sends one short note that resets how extras work, with the scope statement attached. It:

1. Thanks the client for the work they have sent (they have been asking because they value it).
2. Says the founder has written down what the plan covers, so both sides can see it.
3. Says how extras work from now: a quick note with the effort and price before starting, and the goodwill allowance only if the founder chose one. Never offer free work they did not choose.
4. Owns anything the founder owes the client and let slip, in one clause ("the September speed audit slipped on our side; it'll be with you this week"). A reset sent while the founder is behind reads as one-sided; the build checks the note names it.
5. Says nothing about the past extras being owed. Those are gone; billing them now costs the relationship more than the money.
6. Ends warmly and forward.

Example (strong relationship, allowance of two hours):

> Hi Sam, it's been a busy few months on the account, which I love. I've written up what the monthly plan covers so we're both looking at the same list; it's attached. From November, small bits up to two hours a month are on me. Anything bigger, I'll send a quick note with the hours and price before I start, so there are no surprises on either side. Talk on Thursday.

Also answer, in the note, any question the client asked that is still open ("any update on the wholesale thing?"), using the founder's dates and channel.

Rules: 140 words at most, no figures for past work, no "going forward we must", no legal words.

## 8. The goodwill allowance line

The allowance is a gift the client can see. The line that introduces it names the hours and how the founder will flag each use:

> Each month includes up to two hours of small extras at no charge. I'll tell you when one's been used, so you always know where you are.

When a request is covered:

> Happy to. This one's small enough to come out of this month's two goodwill hours, so there's no charge.

## 9. When the client pushes back

Have the founder ready for the three replies that come back. Write one line for each when the relationship is fragile; otherwise mention them in the note.

- **"Can't you just squeeze it in?"** → "I'd rather do it properly than squeeze it. If budget is the issue, I can swap it for [deliverable] this month."
- **"You did it last time."** → "I did, and I was glad to. To keep doing work like that well, I'm putting a price on it from now on." (No apology for the past, no retroactive bill.)
- **"Our last agency included it."** → "That may be right for how they priced things. Here it's [price], or the swap if you'd rather keep the fee where it is."

None of these concede the price. Each offers the swap as the way to say yes without paying.

## 10. Worked replies across tones

Illustrative businesses only.

**Strong relationship, priced (US founder, no tax words on their fee).** A web designer, client asks for a blog section added to a brochure site. Four hours, $480.

> Adding a blog is a smart move for the spring launch. It's a new section rather than part of the five pages we agreed, so it's about four hours, $480, and I'd have it live by the 21st. Want me to go ahead?

**Fragile relationship, swap (UK founder).** A social media manager, client has complained about results and now wants an extra reel. Three hours; swap for a carousel post of the same effort.

> A launch reel makes sense, and I want this month to land well for you. To keep the fee where it is, I can make the reel in place of the second carousel this month. Same effort, more reach for the launch. Want me to do that?

**About to renew, next phase (Australian founder, writes "+ GST" on their fee).** A bookkeeper, client wants monthly management accounts added. Six hours a month.

> Monthly management accounts would give you a much clearer view, and it's a good one to build into next year. Let's put it in the renewal plan for January so it's priced and set up properly from day one. I'll include it in the proposal I send next week.

**Fulfilment drift, priced as a new service.** A coach who sold a done-with-you programme has been writing the client's sales emails for them.

> I can see the emails are the bottleneck, and I'd like to fix that rather than keep patching it. In the programme, you write and I review. If you'd like me to write them for you, that's a done-for-you add-on at £450 a month for four emails. Or I'll run a one-hour session where we build your template together. Which suits you?
