# The VSL flow, section by section

The video replaces a sales call. The order below is the order a good salesperson would
take a prospect through: what I can do for you, why you can trust me, why this is worth
doing, here is the offer and what it costs, here are your questions answered, here is
whether it is for you, here is what to do next, here is proof. Then, for the people
still on the fence, go round again with more proof and more answers.

Timing guides below are for the short version (6 to 8 minutes). Scale the skeptic half
for the long version. Example lines use the fictional bookkeeping offer from the intake
brief. They show shape and register, not words to copy.

---

## Action-taker half

### 1. Hook: what you can help them with (0:00 to 0:20)

**Job.** Make the right person think "I'm in the right place" and the wrong person
leave. Name who it is for and the result, directly.

**Shape.** Who you are. Who this is for. What you help them do. One optional line on
what the video covers.

**Trap.** Warming up. "Hey, thanks for being here, in this video I want to share..."
Every second before the result is a second people leave. Also: no story yet, no
"imagine a world where".

**Example.**
> If you're a bookkeeper with ten or more clients and you're tired of trading hours
> for money, this is for you. My name is Dana Reyes. I help bookkeepers add a monthly
> advisory service that clients pay five hundred to fifteen hundred a month for,
> without learning a new skill or finding new clients. Here's how it works.

### 2. Credibility: why you (0:20 to 0:50)

**Job.** Answer "why should I listen to you about this?" with specifics the viewer can
believe. Keep only the proof that connects to the claim in the hook.

**Shape.** Two to four lines: how long, how many, what result, one milestone. If there
is a backstory, it is one sentence, and only if it is itself proof.

**Trap.** The life story. Where you grew up, why you started the business, your
mission. The viewer wants to know if you can do the thing. Also: round, vague numbers
("thousands of clients") read as invented. Use the real figure.

**Example.**
> I've been a bookkeeper for eleven years. Four years ago I added advisory to my own
> practice and went from forty an hour to clients paying me twelve hundred a month.
> Since then forty-six bookkeepers have gone through this with me. [PLACEHOLDER: average
> advisory income added in 90 days.]

For warm traffic, cut this to one line. They already know.

### 3. Sell the opportunity: the bigger why (0:50 to 2:30)

**Job.** Before you pitch the offer, sell them on the approach. Why this result matters
now. Why this way of getting it beats the things they are doing or considering. This is
where the one-line belief lives, explained.

**Shape.**
1. Say what they are doing now, fairly. Why it makes sense that they tried it.
2. Explain why it fails, as cause and effect. Two or three "because / so / which is
   why" links the viewer could repeat to a friend.
3. Name the other option they are weighing and why it falls short too.
4. State the approach that works and why, with one picture: an example with simple
   numbers, introduced as an example.

**Trap.** Claiming instead of explaining. "The old way doesn't work anymore" is a
claim. Teaching the how. If you give the steps, the viewer thinks "I can do that
myself" and leaves. Say what and why. The steps are the offer.

**Example.**
> Most bookkeepers try one of two things to earn more. Raise the hourly rate, or take
> on more clients. Both make sense. Both hit a wall. Raise the rate and clients push
> back, because to them you're the same service at a higher price. Add clients and
> you add hours, because every client is more of the same work. So the income goes up
> a little and the time goes up a lot.
>
> Some go and get a CFO certification. Six months, eight thousand dollars. And at the
> end you know more... but you still don't have anything new to sell, and you still
> have to go find someone to sell it to.
>
> Here's what works instead. You already produce the three reports a business owner
> needs to make decisions. Cash flow, margins, aging. Right now they sit in a folder.
> Package them into a monthly thirty minute call and you have an advisory service.
> Your clients already trust you with their numbers, so there's no cold selling. Say
> you have fifteen clients and five say yes at seven hundred a month. That's three
> thousand five hundred a month in new income, for about ten hours of work.

### 4. The offer, price first (2:30 to 4:00)

**Job.** Say how you help them do this. Say the price. Then list what comes with it.

**Shape.**
1. Bridge: "Here's how I help you do this."
2. Name the offer.
3. Price, plainly. Payment plan if there is one.
4. Deliverables, one per line. Real items. What it is, how much of it, when.

**Why price first.** Price last is a cliffhanger, and everything before it feels like
a setup. Price first means everything after it justifies the number. It also sends the
wrong people away before they book, which keeps the calendar clean and the sales team
sane. Every real business tells you the price before you buy. Do the same.

**Trap.** The value stack. "The templates alone are worth $3,000, the calls are worth
$5,000, total value $14,000, but today only $4,500." It is not true, it is not
compliant, and the moment the viewer hears it every number before it stops being
believable. List what comes with it. Let the list do the work.

**Example.**
> So here's how I help you do this. It's called Advisory Upgrade. It costs four
> thousand five hundred dollars, or three payments of sixteen fifty. Here's what you
> get. Eight weekly group calls with me, where we build your service week by week.
> The three report templates, done, so you're not building from scratch. A pricing
> calculator that tells you what to charge based on your client's size. A twenty
> minute pitch script for the conversation with your existing clients. Two one-on-one
> reviews with me of your first two pitches before you deliver them. And twelve months
> in the member group, where everyone shares what's working.

### 5. Objections: answer the questions before they ask (4:00 to 5:30)

**Job.** Say the questions people ask when they hear the offer, and answer each one.
Include the price question.

**Shape.** "The most common questions I get at this point are..." then one at a time:
the question in the viewer's words, a direct answer, next question.

**Trap.** Generic objections nobody actually has. Use what prospects say on calls and
in DMs. If there is no call data yet, use the defaults from the intake brief and tell
the person they are guesses. Also: answering with a claim ("don't worry, it works").
Answer with a reason.

**Example.**
> The questions I get most at this point are these.
>
> Why four and a half thousand? Because the first client who says yes at seven
> hundred a month pays it back in seven months, and most people in the program pitch
> their first client in week five.
>
> My clients won't pay more. Some won't, and that's fine. You're not raising the price
> of bookkeeping. You're offering a different thing. In the program we go through
> which of your clients to ask first, and it's usually the three who already call you
> with questions.
>
> I'm not a CFO. You don't need to be. The reports already exist. The call is you
> explaining what the numbers mean, which you do every time a client asks.
>
> How much time does it take? About three hours a week for eight weeks, then thirty
> minutes a month per advisory client.

### 6. Who it's for, who it's not for (5:30 to 6:00)

**Job.** Say plainly who gets results with this and who does not. The wrong people
leave here instead of on a call.

**Shape.** "This is for you if..." two or three lines. "This is not for you if..." two
or three lines. Honest, not clever.

**Trap.** Making "not for" a compliment in disguise ("this isn't for people who aren't
ready to succeed"). Say the real disqualifiers: not enough clients, wants a
certificate, wants it done for them when it is a program.

**Example.**
> This is for bookkeepers with at least ten clients who've been doing this for a few
> years and want to earn more without working more hours. It's not for you if you have
> fewer than five clients, because you need existing clients to sell this to. And it's
> not for you if what you want is a certificate. There's no certificate here. There's
> a service you can sell by week eight.

### 7. Call to action (6:00 to 6:20)

**Job.** One action. What they will see when they scroll down. What happens after.

**Trap.** Two actions ("book a call, or if you're not ready, join my newsletter").
Urgency that is not real. Vague next steps ("my team will reach out").

**Example.**
> If that's you, scroll down and book a thirty minute call with me. Pick a time that
> works. I'll call you at that time, we'll look at your client list together, and
> you'll leave the call knowing whether this makes sense for your practice.

### 8. Proof (6:20 to 7:30)

**Job.** Three or four testimonials the viewer will believe.

**Shape.** In the script, write what each one shows and where it comes from. For a
video testimonial, write the one line it must contain. For a screenshot, note what it
is a screenshot of (a message in the member group, an email, a bank transfer). Show the
person's first name and face when permitted.

**What makes it believable.** The screenshot looks like where it came from. The result
is specific. The person is findable. Polished quote graphics look fake even when they
are real; raw screenshots look real because they are.

**Trap.** Inventing testimonials. If there are none, write
`[PLACEHOLDER: testimonial 1 of 3. Needs: first name, a specific result, screenshot or
video]` and move on. The video can launch with placeholders removed; it cannot launch
with fake proof.

**Example (script notes, not spoken).**
> [On screen: screenshot from the member group, Priya, "first client said yes at $900
> a month, week 5"]
> [On screen: screenshot of email from Marcus, three clients on advisory, $2,400/mo]
> [On screen: 40 second video, Lena, must include the line "I didn't learn anything
> new, I packaged what I already did"]
> [PLACEHOLDER: testimonial 4]

Spoken, one line to introduce them: "Here's what some of the people who've done this
have said."

For the short version, the video ends here with one closing line that repeats the CTA.

## Skeptic half (long version)

Everyone still watching after section 8 is either analytical, cautious, or buying with
money that hurts to lose. They need more reasons, more proof, and a clearer picture of
what happens after they pay. This half can take the video from eight minutes to forty.
Keep the same register: direct, plain, explained.

### 9. Guarantee or refund policy

Only if one exists, and only as written. Say the condition clearly. A conditional
guarantee ("if you pitch five clients using the script and none say yes, I work with
you until one does") is stronger than a blanket one, because it is believable and it
tells the viewer what doing the work looks like.

If there is no guarantee, skip this section. Do not write a softer substitute.

### 10. More proof

Two or three more testimonials, different from section 8. Pick ones that answer the
objections the skeptic still has: someone who doubted it, someone with a small client
list, someone who had tried the certification route.

### 11. More objections: the follow-up method

Every answer creates a new question. The skeptic is the person who asks it. Write the
answer, then ask yourself what they would say next, and answer that.

Example chain:
> "You said sell to existing clients. What if I only have ten and three say no?"
> → answer → "Then what, do I go find new clients?" → answer → "How long does that
> take?" → answer.

Three or four chains of two or three questions each.

### 12. Expectations

What the buyer has to do, week by week in rough shape. What the first thirty days look
like. What a realistic result is and when. What it is not. This is the section that
turns "I hope this works" into "I know what I'm doing on day one", and it also
protects the offer from refund requests later.

> Here's what the first eight weeks look like. Week one you pick your three reports.
> Weeks two and three we build them into your version of the templates. Week four is
> pricing. Week five you pitch your first client, and we review it together first.
> Weeks six to eight you pitch the next ones and we fix what didn't land. Most people
> have one advisory client by week six. Some have three. A few have none and need
> another month. That's normal.

### 13. Proof again

One or two more, or the strongest from section 8 repeated with a line of context.

### 14. Final call to action

Same action as section 7, said once more. Repeat what happens after they click.
Close.

## Sizing guide

| Section | Short (6 to 8 min) | Long (20 to 40 min) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 Hook | 15 to 20 sec | 20 to 30 sec |
| 2 Credibility | 20 to 30 sec | 45 to 60 sec |
| 3 Opportunity | 1.5 to 2 min | 4 to 8 min |
| 4 Offer | 1 to 1.5 min | 2 to 3 min |
| 5 Objections | 1 to 1.5 min | 3 to 5 min |
| 6 For / not for | 20 to 30 sec | 45 sec |
| 7 CTA | 15 sec | 20 sec |
| 8 Proof | 45 to 60 sec | 2 to 3 min |
| 9 to 14 Skeptic half | none | 8 to 20 min |

Sections 1 and 2 together under one minute in both versions. That is where the
drop-off data is worst, and it is nearly always because the speaker took four minutes
to say who they are.

---

## What the data says about watching

Nobody watches to the end. On a well-performing two minute VSL, about 45 percent reach
the end. On a good twelve minute one, the curve declines gently, which means people are
leaving to press the button, not leaving in disgust. On a bad one, 95 percent are gone
in the first fifteen seconds, which means the hook told them it was something they did
not want.

So: front-load. Hook, credibility, opportunity, offer, price, in that order, as fast as
clarity allows. If the viewer leaves at minute four knowing what it is, what it costs
and who it is for, the video did its job. What they did not hear gets delivered after
they book, by the follow-up emails and content.
