# Method

How a lead magnet that founders using this skill can publish today gets made, in the order the work happens.

Contents: 1 Choosing the topic. 2 Choosing the format. 3 Designing a scorecard. 4 Writing a checklist. 5 Writing a one-page guide. 6 The opt-in and thank-you pages. 7 Rebuild for fit. 8 Measuring. 9 What is deliberately left out. 10 Credits.

## 1. Choosing the topic

Five principles decide whether a lead magnet works:

1. **One specific problem, not a subject.** "Which conversation are you avoiding?" beats "a guide to leadership". The test: a buyer would describe the problem in the same words.
2. **Matched to where the reader is.** Someone who has not named the problem needs it named; someone comparing options needs help judging; someone ready to start needs the friction removed.
3. **Worth paying for, quick to use.** Usable in under ten minutes. A reader who does not finish it did not get the value.
4. **One door left open.** It solves a small problem the offer also touches, and leaves exactly one natural next question that the offer answers. Name the door before writing anything.
5. **One format.** A scorecard, or a checklist, or a one-page guide. Never a bundle.

Read the topic from what the founder gave, in this order: the questions people ask before they hire (in their words); the thing people ask for (the slides, the order of work); the problem in their offer page; repeated questions across emails. Count repeats. Rank three candidates, quote the line each came from, recommend one. A topic with no line behind it is "inferred" and the chat says so.

**Never promise the outcome the offer sells.** A scorecard about the cash in the account does not promise more cash. It promises clarity: where the reader stands and what to look at first.

## 2. Choosing the format

| The founder's material | Format | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Readers want to know where they stand; the problem has four to six parts the founder can name (five conversations, five money habits) | Scorecard | Personal result without the founder's time; the answers tell the founder who the reader is |
| The value is an order of steps or a set of things to check (order of work, a pre-launch check) | Checklist | A procedure is followed, not scored; ten to fifteen items fit two pages |
| One idea people get wrong, best shown with one example | One-page guide | One page, one idea, one worked example, one next step |
| Anything needing weeks of the founder's time, a video, a live event, or projected client results | None of these | Out of scope: an ebook is rarely finished, a webinar is an event, a calculator that projects results is a promise |

When two fit, prefer the scorecard where the founder's calls are expensive (it tests fit) and the checklist where the founder cannot host a web page and the topic is a procedure. A scorecard always ships with a printable copy, so hosting never blocks it.

## 3. Designing a scorecard

- **Five scored questions**, about two to three minutes in all. Multiple choice only. Three or four options each, whole-number points, every option scoring differently. A short label above a question can carry the founder's own heading (a slide title) so the question itself stays short.
- **Options describe behaviour, not virtue.** "Raise it, but weeks later than I should" is answerable honestly; "I am a good communicator" is not. The best option is what the founder teaches; when the founder has not said, the option describes the plain version and the chat says it is a draft.
- **Ranges, never exact figures.** Money and volume questions use ranges in the founder's number style with a "Not sure" option. Ranges run low to high with no overlap (checked).
- **Up to two fit questions**, unscored, placed last under "so I can point you to the right next step". Each option is flagged fit or not fit, from the founder's own "who should not call me". With no disqualifier from the founder, add one default question on readiness or size and say so.
- **Tiers.** Three by default, up to five, covering every possible total with no gap or overlap. Named for what is true at that level ("Carrying them quietly"), never a verdict ("Failing", "Red"). Each description is 40 to 70 words and says what is true, what it tends to cost, and where to start.
- **The arithmetic is proved.** Every possible answer set (up to 1,024) is walked. No tier under 10 or over 60 per cent of answer sets; a middle pattern of answers lands in a middle tier. These describe the scoring, not real readers.
- **The result, in this order:** the score out of the maximum; the tier name and description; What is working (one or two lines from the best-scoring questions, or the honest fallback line); Fix these first (three lines from the lowest-scoring questions, ties broken by question order; a question scored in its upper half reads as "tighten"); the door; the call to action, or the not-a-fit line in its place. A reader in the top tier sees the founder's top-tier line and a quiet text link in place of the button, because telling someone with no gaps that "these gaps look familiar" costs the founder their trust. A question answered in its upper half but not at the top shows its `strength_mid` line, so the result never overstates what the reader said.
- **The page.** One self-contained file. Nothing is sent, stored or loaded; no score in any link. Plain by default: one system font, black on white, structure by space and type, no boxes, no rules, no cards. The founder's colours only when they pass contrast. Works by keyboard and on a 375 pixel phone; the result is announced to screen readers; it prints as a clean result.

The fix lines and strength lines are practical first steps the reader can take this week, one sentence each, with no figures.

## 4. Writing a checklist

- Title as "[number]-point [topic] checklist"; the number matches the items ready to publish.
- 10 to 15 items in the founder's order (their order of work, not a generic one). Grouped under short headings above ten.
- Each item: an action in a few words, then one or two sentences of detail. A done box per item. Fits two pages.
- Every item traces to a pasted line, a founder reply, or "founder confirmed". Where the founder contradicts themselves (a self-correction in a voice note), use the corrected version and say so.
- "What this does not cover": one line, which is the door. Then the next step.

## 5. Writing a one-page guide

One idea in two or three sentences; three to five steps; one worked example, labelled as an example (or a client the founder named, in their words); what it does not cover; the next step. About 550 words at most.

## 6. The opt-in and thank-you pages

**Opt-in page, by block:** a headline that promises one specific outcome (not the offer's outcome); a subhead saying what it is and how long it takes; three lines on what they will see; first name and email only (fit is tested inside the magnet, not on the form); a button that names what they get ("Send me the scorecard", never "Download now" or "Submit"); the expectation line (what they get, how often, unsubscribe any time); the founder's sign-off. No download counts, logos or testimonials the founder has not supplied.

The page is built as HTML only when the founder gives the form address or embed code from their own email tool; otherwise the blocks go in the launch pack to paste into any builder, and the chat says why.

**Thank-you page:** confirmation, the link to the magnet, one next step (reply with the score). One next step, not three.

**The flow:** the reader opts in on the founder's form, gets the link on the thank-you page and in email one, and finishes the magnet on its own page. The magnet captures nothing.

## 7. Rebuild for fit

When the last magnet pulled the wrong people:

1. Start from the mismatch: who opted in, which fit answer they gave, and what in the title invited them (usually a title broader than the offer).
2. Tighten or add the fit question that would have caught them, and give its not-fit answer weight (it swaps the call to action).
3. Change the title and the opt-in headline to name the reader the offer is for.
4. A procedure topic can move to a checklist; a "where do I stand" topic stays a scorecard.
5. The pack's section 2 says what the previous magnet was, why it attracted the wrong people, and what changed.

## 8. Measuring

Three counts each week: visits to the opt-in page, opt-ins, readers who finish (or reply); then calls booked. No benchmark: the published conversion figures mostly come from companies selling quiz tools, with no independent source. The founder's first 60 days are the baseline. The rebuild rule: if most opt-ins are the wrong people, change the title and the fit questions, not the traffic.

## 9. What is deliberately left out

Conversion benchmarks; vendor tool lists; ebooks, webinars, courses and calculators; SEO and paid promotion; A/B tests (a small list cannot read one); any consent wording presented as settled.
